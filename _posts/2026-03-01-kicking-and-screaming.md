 _I thought writing a mini-thesis and building a ML fraud detection research project while working full time was going to be the hardest thing I did in 2025, but boy was I wrong._

### Assistant (to the) Regional Manager 
I’d just wrapped up the last PR on the repo and submitted the final report when my manager floated the idea of leading the analytics team I’d been a member of for just over a year by me. 

I’ve never thought of myself as leadership material, and in every career chat I’ve had with managers up to that point, being an individual contributor forever was the only thing I wanted. I’d just invested 5 years of my life completing a CS degree further cementing this fact. I just wanted to stay in my lane - hydrated, moisturised, and working on perfecting the craft of solving hard problems programmatically. Maybe do a PhD on the side eventually.

![A sleepy puppy relaxing in a pond with text: unbothered, moisturized, happy, in my lane, focused, flourishing](/assets/images/in_my_lane.jpg)

My first thought was I’d be a horrible micromanager, (I have very exacting standards) and my second thought, once I’d run a few loops in my mind was… do I really want to move away from the work I’d just spent so many years of my life pursuing in favour of management? As someone who didn’t start out in tech and has painstakingly built a hard-won technical skillset, this gave me serious pause.

But, the allure of the problem got me in the end. The data function had some great, talented people, but wasn’t set up for success. The company had a real desire to be data-driven, but the analytics team was stuck in service desk hell. I’d been vocal about this, and tried in my way to make things better as an analyst. The old restlessness in me stirred (perhaps also some hubris). This was a real hard problem to solve, and this role might give me the scope I needed to attempt it.

### Player-coach hell
I accepted the job, and promptly made every beginner mistake in the book.

In the first few months, I was wary of becoming distant from the work I love: deep thinking, technical problem-solving, and building systems that work. Would this stint in management dilute or erase that part of me?

Because of resource constraints, I was still doing my analyst work. We couldn’t hire another analyst, and I didn’t want the remaining two analysts to burn out. So I bounced between the IC and the manager worlds. Running deep analyses, debugging dbt models, creating competency frameworks, nudging team culture in the right direction, and navigating complex stakeholder relationships. I was doing it all. 

There’s something to be said for player-coach leadership. I was still in the trenches with my team, making the transition from being coworkers to manager and reports a little easier. In my mind, it meant I was keeping my technical edge, and if this management thing didn’t work out after a year, I could always go back to full IC. That sounded good to me. 

Slowly, it began to feel like the team was moving into a new gear too. My management attempts were bearing some fruit.

But I was working longer hours, obsessively checking Slack, and often anxious about the things that were no longer in my direct control.  The constant context switching, high cognitive load of learning a whole new skillset, and high emotional cost of navigating being responsible for other people was beginning to take a toll.

### A false dichotomy
When you are an IC, your contributions get measured in neat units. So many dashboards delivered, so many tickets closed in a sprint, so many happy stakeholders this quarter. Your ability to write good code and deliver useful insights is what makes you valuable.

When you enter into leadership territory, your impact begins to be measured across systems, or years, and the few deliverables you do have aren’t nearly as important as the intangible things like “strategy”, “mentorship” and “delivery”. 

In my mind I was still stuck between either being technically sharp and delivering the best analyses I could, or committing to this nebulous leadership thing and losing the technical edge. I was about to find out the cost of clinging to this false dichotomy.

My crash-and-burn phase unfolded slowly. My projects began to slow down. I was having a hard time concentrating. It was getting harder to regulate my emotions. I lost interest in my hobbies, and was too exhausted to socialize. My life contracted until it was almost exclusively work. 

Burnout. 

At this point, I questioned whether I was cut out for leadership at all.

### On leverage 
Things did not get better immediately. It was hard for me to separate being an effective leader of people doing the thing, and being good at doing the thing. But no way in hell was I going to let this be my Peter Principle point without a fight. 

The burnout forced my hand a bit. I handed over some of the IC work to the other analysts. Instead of being crushed by the workload, they were excited to learn about a new domain and be challenged by it. 

Instead of picking up ad hoc tickets myself when I was unsure of when an analyst would respond, I had us define a service level agreement in our team governing our response times. We were all happier for having clarity.

Instead of picking up more work to keep stakeholders happy and shield the team from overwork, I began saying no more firmly.

I had a few sessions with my manager to align better on his expectations for the type of work I’d take on in my role. I could finally take a few small steps away from the details, and a different perspective clicked into place. 

It wasn’t my job to produce beautiful analytics widgets anymore. It was my job to take what I knew about making analytics widgets, and set up the analytics widget workshop so that other crafts people could produce really good analytics widgets instead. That way, my work can produce far more than it ever could when I was an IC. Leverage! 

![Jesse Pinkman and Walter White from breaking bad captioned: Yeah! Leverage, bitch!](/assets/images/leverage.jpg)

### A redefined ambition
Recently, the question of where I want to be in five years came up.

The restlessness I’ve always felt as an IC has slowly begun to settle down. I used to think that if a system didn’t allow me to produce good work, I could circumvent that by learning more and working harder and being the smartest IC, but it always left me unsatisfied. Now, I want to fix the system, and increasingly, I think that is where my strength actually lies.  

Will my hard-won technical edge dull? - yes. If I’m good at my job, then the analysts on my team will code circles around me. Over time, I will probably lose a lot of the player-coach rapport I have with the people I lead too.  

But my tech skills won’t go to waste; now I apply the mechanism I used to get this first layer of technical skills to problems an order of magnitude larger than anything I used to solve.

In the same way I used to deconstruct particularly gnarly SQL queries, or trace a weird record through an ELT pipeline, now I need to solve problems like empowering teams to get coherent insights from their own data without the data team becoming a bottleneck. Understand the problem from first principles, and iterate and debug the solution till it sticks.  Manage change like I would manage dependencies. 

If working in analytics taught me one thing, it’s that predicting what 5 years from now will look like is often a fool’s errand. Five years ago, I didn’t think I’d be any kind of leader, yet here I am.

For me, these days it makes sense to think of leadership as a form of technical responsibility. The scope of the deliverables might have changed,  but I still value depth, coherence, and long-term thinking.

I don’t know exactly where I’ll be in five years, but I do know I want to be someone whose technical judgment compounds over time.
